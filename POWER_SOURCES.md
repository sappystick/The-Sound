## 1. First-century Aramaic: what survives, and what it says about the Gospel words
**No Galilean Aramaic text from the first century survives.** The nearest witnesses, by period and place:

| Corpus | Date | Dialect | Words here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qumran Aramaic (Dead Sea Scrolls: Genesis Apocryphon, Aramaic Enoch, Targum of Job, Aramaic Levi …) | 2nd c. BC – 1st c. AD | Jewish literary Aramaic of Judaea | 40,027 |
| Biblical Aramaic (Daniel, Ezra) | 6th–2nd c. BC | Imperial / early Jewish | 4,948 |
| Targum Onkelos | 2nd–3rd c. AD (Judean origin) | Jewish literary | 83,102 |
| Jerusalem Talmud (sample) | 4th–5th c. AD | **Galilean**: the dialect of Jesus' region, later | 78,687 |
| Syriac New Testament (Peshitta) | 2nd–5th c. AD | Eastern (Edessa), a translation | 109,640 |

Hebrew in the Dead Sea Scrolls (368,000 words) is a control. Short consonant strings collide with ordinary Hebrew words, so a Hebrew hit is not an Aramaic attestation.

**The Gospel's Aramaic words in each corpus** (per 100,000 words; the form with or without the prefixes w-, d-, l-, b-):

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| *rabbouni* רבוני (Jn 20:16) | 10 | **172** | 5 | 0 | The targumic "my lord" (ribboni). Absent from the Syriac. **Western, Palestinian** (K: Black; McNamara) |

**Results:**
- **Calc / K:** the Aramaic words kept in the Gospels match **Western, Palestinian Jewish Aramaic**: the targums, the Galilean Talmud and, for several, Qumran. They do not match the Eastern Syriac. *Rabbouni* and *korban* are absent from the Syriac altogether.
- ***Abba* is colloquial.** It is missing from the literary Aramaic of Qumran but frequent in Galilean.
- **Language of the power words: Galilean or Palestinian spoken Aramaic.** This is the language the Gospels preserve at the moments of power:
  - *talitha koum*, *ephphatha*, *abba*, *Eloi … sabachthani*;
  - John's *rabbouni* at the tomb.
- **The narrative around them is Greek** (round 46).
- **For reconstructing sound:** the period's own spelling of "I am" is אנה הוא, *ʾanā hū*, attested at Qumran. Our earlier Aramaic "I am" (round 41) used this form.

## 2. Ancient ways of writing sound (sources read this round, and those from the literature)
### Read in the primary text this round
| Source | Date | What it writes down |
|---|---|---|
| **Mishnah Rosh Hashanah 4:9** | compiled c. AD 200; Temple practice | **Rhythm as ratios in words:** "a *tekiah* is as long as three *teruot*; a *teruah* is as long as three whimpers"; three sets of three blasts. A complete rhythmic code with no notation |
| **Mishnah Arakhin 2:3–6** | same | **The ensemble rule:** at least 2 trumpets, at least 9 lyres, no upper limit; **one cymbal only**; at least 12 Levite singers. Boys sang "to give flavour" with high voices but did not play |
| **Mishnah Tamid 7:3** | same | **The liturgy as a score:** two silver trumpets sound *tekiah, teruah, tekiah*; the cymbal; the Levites sing the psalm of the day, **divided into three sections; at each break a *tekiah*, and the people bow down**. Sound cues the body |
| **Mishnah Tamid 3:8** | same | Heard in Jericho: the great gate opening, the *magrepha*, Ben Katin's water-wheel, the crier's voice, the flute, Ben Arza's cymbal, the Levites' song, the shofar. Physics in section 3 |
| **Bavli Arakhin 10b–11a** | 3rd–5th c. | **The *magrepha*:** "one cubit wide, one cubit tall, a handle; hollow, ten holes, each giving ten kinds of tone: one hundred." A baraita says a hundred each, a thousand |
| **Bavli Berakhot 62a** | same | **Hand-signs as score (cheironomy):** "one points to the cantillation notes (טעמי) of the Torah with the right hand" |
| **Josephus, *Antiquities* 7.305–306** | AD 93 | David's songs in "trimeters and pentameters"; the *kinnor* has **10 strings, struck with a plectrum**; the *nabla* has **12 notes, played with the fingers**; cymbals broad, large, of bronze |

### From the literature (not fetched here; standard references)
| System | Date | What survives |
|---|---|---|
| **Babylonian tuning texts** (UET VII 126; CBS 10996) | c. 1800–1400 BC | Names for the nine strings of the lyre and procedures for retuning between seven scales. **String 5, the middle one, is called *qablītum*, "the middle"** (Kilmer 1971; West 1994) |
| **Hurrian hymn h.6, Ugarit** | c. 1400 BC | The oldest notated melody: a hymn text with interval names and numbers below it (Laroche 1955; Kilmer; West 1994) |
| **Euripides, *Orestes* papyrus** (P.Vindob. G 2315) | c. 200 BC | Greek vocal and instrumental notation over a tragic chorus |
| **Delphic hymns** | 128 BC | Greek vocal notation; **the melody follows the word accents** (K) |
| **Seikilos epitaph** | 1st c. AD | A complete short song with notation and rhythm signs, carved on stone |
| **Egyptian cheironomy** | Old Kingdom onward | Tomb reliefs of singers' hand signs facing harpists (Hickmann 1958) |
| **Masoretic accents** (te'amim) | written c. AD 9th–10th; Talmud cites the practice (above) | A full system of melody and syntax over the Hebrew text. They are in the WLC data used here |

**Reading:** across these systems the same layers recur:
- the words;
- a pitch layer (letters, accents, hand signs or interval names);
- a rhythm layer (ratios, strokes, dots);
- cues to the body (bowing at the *tekiah*, facing directions in PGM XIII);
- instruments with the words written on them (1QM).

The middle string is named *the middle* in Babylon (*qablītum*) and in Greece (*mese*).

## 3. Physics of the claims
**"Heard in Jericho" (Tamid 3:8).**
- Jerusalem to Jericho is about 22 km, and 1,000 m downhill.
- Assumptions: a quiet night (20 dB), air absorption by frequency, the hearing threshold, and either plain spreading or a night temperature inversion that channels the sound (spherical spreading to 1 km, cylindrical beyond).

| Band | Source power needed, plain | Under an inversion |
|---|---|---|
| 63 Hz | 135 dB | 122 dB |
| 125 Hz | 129 dB | **115 dB** |
| 250 Hz | 140 dB | 126 dB |
| 500 Hz | 160 dB | 146 dB |

| Source (estimated sound power) | Audible in Jericho? |
|---|---|
| The crier's voice, a great shout (97 dB) | no |
| The Levites' choir of 12 (108 dB) | no |
| A shofar (115 dB) | marginal, at 125 Hz under an inversion |
| **Two silver trumpets (118 dB)** | **yes, low band (125 Hz), on a still night with an inversion** |
| **Ben Arza's cymbal (120 dB peak)** | **yes, 125 Hz, same conditions** |
| **The great gate slammed (≈125 dB)** | **yes, 63–125 Hz, same conditions** |
| 120 trumpets (2 Chr 5:12) | yes, 63–250 Hz |

**Calc:**
- Under plain spreading, none of them reaches Jericho.
- On a still night with a temperature inversion, the low, loud, sudden sounds can: the trumpets, the cymbal, the gate.
- The voices cannot: the crier and the Levites' song.
- **The Mishnah's list is half physically possible.** The plausible half is exactly the loud low impulses.

**The *magrepha* (Calc, Reading):**
- **Reading of the text:** "ten holes, each ten kinds of tone, one hundred" read as **ten pipes, each overblown through ten harmonics**.
- **The pipe set:** ten stopped pipes for the planetary heptachord (165–293 Hz) plus the next three notes up.
  - They need lengths up to **0.52 m**.
  - **They fit in a box one royal cubit (0.525 m) high, not one common cubit (0.445 m).**
- **The pitches:** ten pipes × ten harmonics give 45 distinct semitone pitches, so "a hundred kinds of tone" is the count of pipe-harmonic combinations.
- **K:** the *magrepha* is otherwise an unsolved puzzle. The Talmud debates whether it was a shovel or a sounding instrument. Later sources call it an organ.

## 4. What this adds to the picture
1. **The language layers.**
   - The power words were spoken in **Western, Palestinian (Galilean) Aramaic**, the colloquial kind, as *abba* shows.
   - The Gospel narrative was composed in **Greek** (round 46).
   - Scripture behind both was **Hebrew**, sung with cantillation.
2. **The score layers already existed in Jesus' world, without staff notation:**
   - rhythm as ratios (Rosh Hashanah 4:9);
   - sound cues for the body (Tamid 7:3);
   - inscribed instruments (1QM);
   - performance directions over the text (psalm titles);
   - hand signs (Berakhot 62a);
   - a named middle string (*qablītum*, *mese*).
3. **The Temple's instruments are specified:**
   - 10-string *kinnor* with a plectrum and 12-note *nabla* with the fingers (Josephus);
   - at least 2 trumpets and 9 lyres, one cymbal, 12 voices (Mishnah);
   - a possible 10-pipe *magrepha* that fits a royal cubit.

   **Next:** combine these with the seven-string lyre and vowel tubes of rounds 41–44 into a reconstructed Temple ensemble, and render Tamid 7:3 as a score: trumpets *tekiah–teruah–tekiah*, the cymbal, the psalm in three sections, a *tekiah* at each break.

## 5. Round 48: the daily Temple service rendered as a score
**Files:**
- script `power/round48_tamid_service.py`;
- numbers `power/round48_results.json`;
- audio **`power/audio/r48_tamid_service_psalm24.mp3`** (2 min 53 s).

**The score, step by step from the Mishnah:**
1. **Opening call:** two priests with silver trumpets sound *tekiah – teruah – tekiah* (Tamid 7:3).
   - The lengths follow the ratios of Rosh Hashanah 4:9: a *tekiah* equals 3 *teruot*, and a *teruah* equals 3 "whimpers".
   - At one whimper = 0.3 s, that gives a *teruah* of three short blasts (0.9 s) and a *tekiah* of 2.7 s.
2. **The cymbal:** Ben Arza strikes it, and only one is allowed (Arakhin 2:5).
3. **The psalm:** the Levites sing the psalm of the day. On Sunday this is Psalm 24, "The earth is the LORD's" (Tamid 7:4).
   - Twelve voices (Arakhin 2:6).
   - Lyres and harps: the *kinnor* of 10 strings and the *nabla* of 12 notes (Josephus).
4. **The breaks:** the psalm falls in three sections. At each break the trumpets sound a *tekiah* and the people bow, rendered here as 2 s of silence.

**Choices that are ours, not the text's:**
- **Section breaks:** 24:1–2 / 3–6 / 7–10. These follow the psalm's own turns: creation, then "who may ascend", then "lift up your heads".
- **Sound:** a whimper of 0.3 s; a trumpet pitch of 233 Hz.
- **Pronunciation:** Tiberian-type Hebrew, which is a later reconstruction.
- **Space:** the reverberation of a stone court.

**Not used:** the *magrepha* is not part of this rite in Tamid 7:3.

## 6. Round 49: the same service played on the seven-string lyre with vowel tubes
**Files:**

- audio **`power/audio/r49_tamid_psalm24_lyre_tubes.mp3`** (2 min 22 s).

**What changes:** the trumpets and cymbal stay as in round 48. Psalm 24 is now played, not sung. Each of its 196 syllables plucks the string of its vowel (Nicomachus) through that vowel's tube.

**String shares:**

| String | hypate (Saturn) | parhypate | lichanos | mese | trite | paranete | nete |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Share | **25%** | 4.6% | 13.3% | 12.8% | 6.6% | **26.5%** | 11.2% |

**What it shows:**
- **Hebrew sounds all seven strings.**
- The lowest string (Saturn), which Koine Greek leaves silent (round 45), carries a quarter of the psalm, through the vowel *qamets* (ɔ).
- **Caveat:** the open-o pronunciation of *qamets* is Tiberian (medieval). Second Temple Hebrew probably had a long ā there, which would move these syllables to the Moon string.

**Our choices:** u is played on the υ string and the short ə on ε.
