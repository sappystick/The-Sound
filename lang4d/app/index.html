What KANON is

      
Every KANON program has five readings: text, speech, music, a process and a figure. Seven vowels are seven planes of data and seven strings of a monochord. Twelve consonants are twelve operations on the 4 × 3 grid of the breastpiece. A syllable is one of 84 cells.

      
Order carries meaning at three scales. Syllables in a word run as a pipeline. Words in a verse run in ring order: the outer pair first and the centre last. Verses run in sequence. Each order has its own rule, so a reordering is a different process. It produces a different result whenever the operations interact. Operations that commute (two additions) give the same result in either order, as they must in any usable language.

      
No imports. The Canon, a built-in library of 44 names on the grid, covers number theory, statistics, geometry, sequences, logic, music, Greek and Hebrew numerals, time and checksums. A prelude written in KANON itself supplies multiplication, Euclid's algorithm and the factorial. With names, guards and while-loops the language is Turing complete: the planes work as the registers of a counter machine.

      
Speaking is compiling. The audio codec turns a program into tones and back. Every verse can end with five short parity words, a Reed–Solomon code over the 113-element field that matches the spoken symbols. It repairs any two misheard symbols in a block and always detects three or four. In tests on 120 random programs, every one came back exact at 4 dB signal-to-noise. At 3 dB, 117 came back exact and 3 were flagged. At 2 dB, correction raised the exact count from 12 to 17. No wrong program passed silently at any noise level.

      
The memory aids are ancient. The planetary week gives the vowels their days, and every weekday name still follows it. The months of the year, in four seasons of three, give the consonants their rooms in a memory palace. Ring composition gives the reading order.

      
"One night" is a design target. The course has 30 steps of about 4 minutes each, but it has not yet been tested on learners. The page's interpreter (JavaScript) agrees with the reference interpreter (Python) on 1,000 of 1,000 random programs and on every course example.
